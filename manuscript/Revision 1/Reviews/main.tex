\begin{enumerate}

\item {\it While the development of a general methodology for manifold estimation is worthwhile, you need to give more thought to how to make this general approach more suitable for the specific application. For example, you note that your method struggles to get the shape of the hippocampus because the true shape has sharp curves in it.  Couldn't/shouldn't your methodology be modified to take account of this feature of the hippocampus?  As an analogy, when using cubic splines to fit one-dimensional curves, one can get curves that aren't differentiable by putting more than one knot at a single location.  There should be some way of doing something similar for manifold estimation.  I think it would be very interesting to try some (perhaps ad hoc) modification of your penalty function that would allow you to better fit this aspect of the hippocampus shape.}

{\bf Response:}
We appreciate your thoughts on this topic and we considered this issue at length when writing the manuscript. A key point of discussion throughout our work was how to balance creating a method that met the needs of our specific application with the goal of developing an approach that would be useful in additional settings. Our testing of the proposed algorithm on a variety of structures indicates that the likely reason the proposed method struggled to fit the shape of the hippocampus was due to the presence of intersections in the surface. Our data augmentation approach did not successfully eliminate these intersections. Importantly, this issue did not seem to be caused by the smoothing introduced during the manifold estimation process. While it may be possible to circumvent this by adapting the penalty function, we believe that properly eliminating self-intersections in all settings will more directly address the underlying issue causing the poor fit. Partitioning of the closed surface in such cases is a standard approach integrated in many principal manifold estimation techniques. As a result, we have focused our efforts on partitioning the hippocampus data to definitively remove self-intersections. We discuss cases in which the data augmentation approach may be insufficient in Section 3.3, including the following:

\revision{However, it is important to note that because this augmentation relies on the use of polar or spherical coordinates to avoid self-intersections, it will not be appropriate in some situations. If the structure of interest strays too far from a circular or spherical shape, the added dimensions may also include intersections. While these cases could be addressed via further augmentation, the partitioning approach described above serves as a reliable option to avoid the curse of dimensionality.} 

Additionally, we have restructured the analysis of simulation case 8 to compare the data augmentation and partitioning approaches directly using spherical data. These changes are shown throughout Section 4, and the results demonstrating that augmentation results in some inconsistencies in manifold fit even when used with spherical data while the estimates from partitioned data appear to faithfully recover the true manifold. However, in this setting, the volume estimates obtained from partitioned data show higher error than those found using augmentation. In summary, the advantage of data augmentation is that it results in better estimation of the volumes compared to the partitioning approach, however, further investigation into robustness of the data augmentation approach is needed. We add a note on this in Section 6. 

We also updated our analysis of the ADNI data to include the partitioning approach, and a comparison of the results of the two processing approaches is given in Section 5 of the revised manuscript.

\item {\it Your methodological development is highly algorithmic with no actual model for the shapes of brain regions or for the measurement process.  In your simulations, it looks like your simulated data includes independent measurement error (but you need to make that clear).  Given the pre-processing steps in your data analysis, I suspect that the actual ``errors" in your observation are spatially dependent.  If so, then you need to discuss this issue and do some simulations that try to more realistically mimic the measurement process in your actual data.}

{\bf Response:} We agree that the issue of data generation process is of utmost importance in this case. Depending on factors including the voxel size and the application of image pre-processing steps that reduce systematic noise while retaining random noise, the dependence structure in the added noise may be considered negligible. In fact, this assumption is made in a wide variety of imaging studies. We completely agree with the Editor, it is likely that the measurement errors induced by the segmentation process are spatially dependent. We have taken steps to account for this further in our simulations, allowing us to evaluate robustness of our methods to inclusion of moderate dependencies in the noise structure. In the previous version of the manuscript, we had included spatially-dependent errors by allowing the underlying manifold function at a given time point to randomly deviate from the true manifold. Because the function defining the manifold has changed, two points sampled from that manifold with close proximity will tend to err from the true manifold in similar ways. To further incorporate considerations of spatially-dependent errors into the simulation models and highlight their effects better, in the revised manuscript we have also updated the data generation process to allow the level of observation-level noise to vary according to the observation's position along the manifold. Specifically, observation-level noise is drawn from a mean-zero Gaussian distribution with variance specified by a Gaussian process with mean 1 and a squared exponential kernel. Thus, the extent to which observations deviate from the image-specific manifold function is also dependent on the location of the observation along the kernel. This change is described in the following change to Section 4.1 of the revised manuscript:

\revision{We incorporate several sources of systematic variability to reflect the patterns of noise commonly observed in imaging data. In these functions, we specify $\alpha = 1 - \zeta\mathbf{g}(t) + \upsilon_{\text{a}}$ and $\beta = 1 + \upsilon_{\text{p}}$, where $\zeta$ and the functional form of $g(\cdot)$ are varied between visits to represent structural changes in the underlying manifold over time. The function $g(\cdot)$ may show change with respect to time that is constant ($g(t) = 0$), linear ($g(t) = t$), or quadratic ($g(t) = t^2$) with $\zeta$ serving as a scalar multiplier. The values $\upsilon_{\text{a}}$ and $\upsilon_{\text{p}}$ are randomly sampled from a normal distribution with mean zero and variance that is varied between simulation runs. Finally, the $\iota$ represent within-image noise in the high-dimensional space, and are randomly sampled from a normal distribution with mean zero. To account for the potential of spatially-dependent errors in the image segmentation process, the variance of this noise distribution varies according to a Gaussian process with mean 1 and using the squared exponential kernel, with the variance differing by simulation run and the bandwidth parameter set to 1. Note that the values $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can intuitively be considered size and shape parameters for the generated structure, respectively. Thus, variability in these terms represents measurement error that may result in misestimation of the structure size and shape. In addition to injecting variability in the estimated shapes of the simulated structures, we also allow the time points for all follow-up visits to vary according to a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 0.01, reflecting the fact that study visits in imaging studies do not occur at exact intervals in practice.}

Finally, inclusion of spatially-dependent noise in the data generation model is an important methodological problem. Given restrictions of page limits, we believe this is out of the scope of our current work. However, we include a comment on this in Section 6 noting that this is an important future research direction as follows. 

\revision{Additional work may also consider alternative observation-level dependencies. In higher resolution imaging studies, a higher degree of dependence may be assumed between the intensities of voxels in the same neighborhood. Thus, investigation of including spatially-dependent noise in the proposed LPME framework is an important future research direction}.

\item {\it Your criterion function (4) includes terms with derivatives in space and another with a derivative in time, but no terms that include mixed partials in space and time.  I am wondering what are the implications of this choice and how much your result would change if you included some kind of integral of a squared mixed partial derivative in (4).  I'm not sure how far you would want to go down this avenue, but I do think it is at least worth acknowledging that the form of (4) is somewhat arbitrary and that other forms might be worth considering.  I would also note that (4) puts no penalty on large linear trends in time, which may not be biologically realistic.}

{\bf Response:} The idea of including mixed partial derivatives in space and time in the criterion function is an interesting proposal which would have important implications for the interpretation of the minimization problem being addressed in this paper and the resulting embedding function. We considered various penalties during our work on this manuscript. We made the decision to include derivatives with respect to space and time separately in the criterion function for several reasons. First, each of these derivatives individually represents a property of the expected behavior of manifolds in the neuroimaging setting: manifolds should display a certain level of smoothness (described by the spatial derivative), and because we are using the manifold to represent a structure in the brain, we expect changes in the manifold over time (represented by the temporal derivative). In the neuroimaging setting, we also found it advantageous to include these derivatives separately because the extent of a manifold's spatial smoothness may differ substantially from the extent of its temporal smoothness, and the two may not necessarily be related. For example, a function fitting to the surface of the hippocampus must show a relatively high level of curvature, but in a healthy aging individual this function should show minimal change over time. In this case, incorporating these partial derivatives separately allows us to effectively distinguish between spatial roughness and temporal smoothness, or vice versa. By including mixed partial derivatives in space and time, the criterion function would effectively be penalizing joint change in space and time. While there are certainly situations in which this would be a valuable consideration, in this setting we expect that these mixed derivatives would work to counteract the separation between space and time that we believe to be useful (e.g. in this application) since the disparities between spatial and temporal smoothness may be large. 

With this in mind, we expect that including the mixed partial derivatives would potentially result in meaningful changes in the results we yielded by the LPME algorithm. Namely, additional smoothing would be applied, mitigating any relatively quick changes in the estimated manifolds between nearby time points. Whether this additional smoothing is desirable would likely depend on the signal-to-noise ratio of the data: does the error in estimates of subcortical structures caused by the imaging and segmentation processes outweigh any real changes in the structure of interest? It is also unclear how much of the additional smoothing provided by including the additional mixed partial derivatives would not otherwise be included by increasing the tuning parameter values for either spatial or temporal smoothing in the LPME algorithm as proposed in the manuscript. An additional consideration is that the inclusion of mixed partial derivatives would likely make the algorithm more sensitive to the relationship between the units of time and space being used. While we standardized all dimensions in the observed data to fall between 0 and 1, it is not guaranteed that the roughness in time and space will occur over the same scale, even if it is present to some extent. This effect should be possible to avoid through careful preprocessing, but the possibility of dependency between spatial and temporal smoothness makes this preprocessing much more important for the success of the algorithm.

We add a note on the model presentation in Section 3.1 (AE: maybe add a sentence saying a mixture of partial derivatives could be added in future work.)

\revision{While this is a useful distinction in the neuroimaging context, in other settings, it may be valuable to consider mixed partial derivatives in space and time, an addition that may be pursued in future work.}

Finally, you note that the criterion function does not penalize large linear trends in time. We agree that these large linear trends in time are likely not biologically realistic. However, in our algorithmic approach, we attempted to place minimal assumptions on the form of the manifold and the temporal changes in that form. While we agree that we are unlikely to observe large linear changes in hippocampal structure over time, we feel we do not have enough prior information to explicitly encode those assumptions in the algorithm. The approach we have taken, which penalizes spatial and temporal roughness at a high level, remains flexible, accommodating a range of manifold forms while encouraging biological plausibility. 

We now add the following in Section 6 to encourage further investigation. 

\revision{It may be useful to explore alterations to the criterion function given in Equation 4 that enforce a different set of assumptions if, for example, stronger prior beliefs exist regarding the nature of changes in the target manifold.}

\end{enumerate}

\large{\textbf{Response to Referee 2}}

\vspace{0.25in}

{\it The authors of this manuscript propose and evaluate a longitudinal principal manifold estimation method and a novel data augmentation approach to enable principal manifold estimation on self-intersecting manifolds. The developed methodology is relatively computationally efficient and provides good approximation for the changes in the brain volume structures. The proposed method, LPME, is also applied to the data obtained from the longitudinal ADNI study.  The work undertaken is interesting and the new method and its application to the ADNI study data is a worthwhile undertaking. However, there are a few issues that should be addressed.}


\begin{enumerate}

\item {\it  First, the described methodology depends on a relatively strong assumption that the underlying manifold is minimally changing over time and the changes can be well described by a smooth time function. }

{\bf Response:} 

Consideration of the assumptions made by our modeling approach is crucial to understanding the situations in which it may be appropriately applied. We distinguish between the assumptions that the underlying manifold is minimally changing over time and that these changes can be well described using a smooth function with respect to time. While the smoothing spline functions that we use impose the assumption of smoothness, they do not require the changes in the underlying manifold over time to be minimal. As we seek to demonstrate in our simulations in Section 4 through the incorporation of a function to describe temporal change in the underlying manifold, denoted by $g(t)$, the proposed approach can accommodate longitudinal change of varying magnitudes, as long as the functions dictating this change are indeed smooth.

While the proposed methodology does not require that changes in the underlying manifold are minimal, we believe that this characteristic is common in the subcortical surfaces we model. The healthy adult brain does exhibit changes as it ages, but these changes tend to be minor in terms of the overall structure of the brain,  and are felt over the span of years. In the case of the hippocampus, a review conducted by \citet{fjell2014What} finds support for annual atrophy rates of 0.79-2.0\% in the hippocampus for healthy aging study participants, well below the magnitude of change included in the simulations in Section 4. While this atrophy will be accelerated for those with AD, we expect the magnitude of change to remain within the bounds set by the simulations.

We address these points in the revised manuscript by including the following in Section 3.1:

\revision{By penalizing the spatial and longitudinal roughness in the underlying manifold, we effectively assume that the target manifold itself, as well as any changes in the manifold, must be describable using smooth functions.}

\item {\it Second, it is not clear if the method proposed applies only to discrete time points or if it can be used in the continuous time setting, i.e. with irregular time intervals.}

{\bf Response:} The proposed method can theoretically be used in any situation in which a smoothing spline is appropriate; thus, it is applicable in a continuous time setting. To demonstrate this, we have added random variability to the time points in the simulations, creating irregular time intervals. We have also added the following comment in Section 6:

\revision{Because this method uses a smoothing spline function to estimate longitudinal change in the underlying manifold, it can be applied to cases where time points are observed at discrete intervals or are randomly sampled from a continuous time interval.}

\item  {\it Third, the simulation studies summarized indicate that the new method is advantageous is specific simulation settings. Are such settings relevant to the brain structure changes?}

{\bf Response:} One of the key ways in which manifold learning methods differ from one another is in the properties they assume the target manifold to have. Thus, it is common to evaluate the performance of manifold learning algorithms a collection of manifolds that may exhibit the assumed properties to varying degrees. Because PME-based methods depend on smoothing splines to estimate the target manifold, we are particularly interested in understanding how these methods perform with varying amounts of smoothness in the underlying structure. The simulation cases we considered were selected to reflect this interest, and most of these examples do not relate to the brain structures of interest beyond this.

The important exception to this is case 8, in which observations are sampled from the surface of a sphere. Fitting the surface of a sphere holds many similarities to fitting the surfaces of subcortical structures, particularly those like the thalamus, which have a roughly spherical shape. In addition to the standard analysis conducted for the other simulation cases, for case 8 in particular we designed our analysis to reflect the steps we took in analyzing the ADNI data in Section 5. Of particular interest, we used the manifold estimates obtained using the PME and LPME algorithms to estimate the volumes of the simulated spheres with the goal of measuring how accurately these methods could recover the volume of the true underlying spheres used to generate the data. We believe that these results help to support the analysis conducted in Section 5.

We include the following discussion of these factors in Section 4 of the revised manuscript.

\revision{As discussed in Section 3, two important assumptions of the proposed approach are that the target manifold may be described using a smooth function, and that longitudinal changes in the manifold are also smooth. To assess the effects of these assumptions, we selected simulation cases that would exhibit these properties to varying extents.}

Additionally, we include the following exploration of simulation case 8 to assess the accuracy with which we can recover the true volume of the simulated spheres, as follows:

\revision{Of particular interest is simulation case 8, in which the simulated data takes the form of a sphere with $D = 3$. Because $t \geq 0$ for all simulated visits, the linear and quadratic forms of $g(\cdot)$ result in a sphere with a decreasing radius, approximating atrophy in a subcortical structure. To examine differences in the two approaches to avoiding self-intersections, the manifold estimation process was conducted twice for each simulated dataset in this case -- once with the data being augmented by spherical coordinates as described in Section \ref{ss:selfInt}, and once with the data split into two partitions. Each set of manifold estimates was then used to obtain an approximation of the spherical volume. For both augmented and partitioned datasets, volume can be estimated by using the \texttt{alphashape3d} package in \texttt{R} to approximate the $\alpha$-shape containing the projections of the observed data onto the estimated manifold, and computing the volume \citep{lafarge2023alphashape3d}. Additionally, if the manifolds were estimated using partitioned data, a Monte Carlo simulation generated points within a three-dimensional space of known volume, and the interior identification approach described in Section 8 of \cite{meng2021Principal} determined the proportion of points belonging to the sphere's interior.}

\item \textit{Section 3, p.9:    Justification for the use of manifolds to learn about the volume changes is not fully satisfactory as volumes are scalar quantities. One does not need to estimate the surface changes in order to obtain a volume of a 3D object.}

{\bf Response:} We agree, it is not necessary to estimate the surface of a structure to approximate its volume. However, volume estimation provides a limited summary of the structure; it is just one of many radiomic features that can be computed after identification of the surface of the region of interest. For instance, \cite{aerts2014decoding} describe over 400 features automatically computed from tumor images for prediction of survival for people with different types of cancer. These radiomic features include characteristics of the shape of the tumor such as tumor volume, surface volume, surface smoothness estimators, among others. Similar radiomic approaches have been used in AD research to compute quite similar radiomic features of regions of interest for AD diagnosis and cognitive score prediction. Estimating the surface structure correctly has immense benefits in terms of computing all these radiomic features that extend beyond volume estimation. Namely, the volume of a subcortical structure like the hippocampus is a blunt summary of its shape, while estimating the surface of the structure provides a much more informative description. Given the importance of the hippocampus in functions related to memory and learning, we believe that characterizing \textit{how} the hippocampus atrophies in those with Alzheimer's disease may be as important as quantifying the extent of this atrophy, and the manifold estimates we obtain are better suited to answering this question compared with generally used methods that rely on the region volume. Due to the greater focus on hippocampal volume at the present time, we decided to address a problem with a direct impact on this line of work while establishing an approach that can easily extend to more informative measures of hippocampal change in future work. We now update the manuscript by including comments on other features that may be computed using our approach to surface estimation. We also include references to work emphasizing the importance of accurately estimating the surface of the region. In addition, in our response to Comment \# 7 of Referee \# 1, we discuss estimation of the average surface trajectories for populations of interest and performing statistical inference on comparisons of these trajectories. As discussed, we now include a reference to this in Section 6 and we plan to expand on these issues in future research.

\item \textit{Table 2, p.23 and Table S1: While in many simulation settings (Cases), the LPME algorithm seems to perform well. Case 7 looks to be troublesome. Any specific reason why in this case PC/PS method performs the best on average?}
