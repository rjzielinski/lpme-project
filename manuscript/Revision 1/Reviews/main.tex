\large{\textbf{Response to Editor}}

\vspace{0.3in}

\begin{enumerate}

\item {\it While the development of a general methodology for manifold estimation is worthwhile, you need to give more thought to how to make this general approach more suitable for the specific application. For example, you note that your method struggles to get the shape of the hippocampus because the true shape has sharp curves in it.  Couldn't/shouldn't your methodology be modified to take account of this feature of the hippocampus?  As an analogy, when using cubic splines to fit one-dimensional curves, one can get curves that aren't differentiable by putting more than one knot at a single location.  There should be some way of doing something similar for manifold estimation.  I think it would be very interesting to try some (perhaps ad hoc) modification of your penalty function that would allow you to better fit this aspect of the hippocampus shape.}

{\bf Response:}
We appreciate your thoughts on this topic, as a key point of discussion throughout our work was how to balance creating a method that met the needs of our specific application with the goal developing an approach that would be useful in additional settings. Our testing of the proposed algorithm on a variety of structures indicates that the likely reason we struggled to fit the shape of the hippocampus was due to the presence of intersections in the surface that our data augmentation approach did not successfully eliminate, rather than the smoothing introduced during the manifold estimation process. While it may be possible to circumvent this by adapting the penalty function, we believe that properly eliminating self-intersections in all settings will more directly address the underlying issue causing the poor fit. 

As a result, we have focused our efforts on partitioning the hippocampus data to definitively remove self-intersections. We discuss cases in which the data augmentation approach may be insufficient in Section 3.3, including the following:

\revision{However, it is important to note that because this augmentation relies on the use of polar or spherical coordinates to avoid self-intersections, it will not be appropriate in some situations. If the structure of interest strays too far from a circular or spherical shape, the added dimensions may also include intersections. In these cases, the partitioning approach described above serves as a reliable option.} 

Additionally, we have restructured the analysis of simulation case 8 to compare the data augmentation and partitioning approaches directly using spherical data. These changes are shown throughout Section 4, and the results demonstrating that augmentation results in some inconsistencies in manifold fit even when used with spherical data while the estimates from partitioned data appear to faithfully recover the true manifold. However, in this setting, the volume estimates obtained from partitioned data show higher error than those found using augmentation.

We also updated our analysis of the ADNI data to include the partitioning approach, and a comparison of the results of the two processing approaches is given in Section 5.

\item {\it Your methodological development is highly algorithmic with no actual model for the shapes of brain regions or for the measurement process.  In your simulations, it looks like your simulated data includes independent measurement error (but you need to make that clear).  Given the pre-processing steps in your data analysis, I suspect that the actual "errors" in your observation are spatially ependent.  If so, then you need to discuss this issue and do some simulations that try to more realistically mimic the measurement process in your actual data.}

{\bf Response:} Thank you for your thoughts on this topic. We agree that it is likely for the measurement errors induced by the segmentation process to be spatially dependent, and we have taken steps to account for this in our simulations. In the first version of the manuscript we submitted, we included spatially-dependent errors by allowing the underlying manifold function at a given time point to randomly deviate from the true manifold. Because the function defining the manifold has changed, two points sampled from that manifold with close proximity will tend to err from the true manifold in similar ways. To further incorporate considerations of spatially-dependent errors into the simulation models, we have also updated the data generation process to allow the level of observation-level noise to vary according to the observation's position along the manifold. Specifically, observation-level noise is drawn from a mean-zero Gaussian distribution with variance specified by a Gaussian process with mean 1 and a squared exponential kernel. Thus, the extent to which observations deviate from the image-specific manifold function is also dependent on the location of the observation along the kernel. This change is described in the following change to Section 4.1:

\revision{We incorporate several sources of systematic variability to reflect the patterns of noise commonly observed in imaging data. In these functions, we specify $\alpha = 1 - \zeta\mathbf{g}(t) + \upsilon_{\text{a}}$ and $\beta = 1 + \upsilon_{\text{p}}$, where $\zeta$ and the functional form of $g(\cdot)$ are varied between visits to represent structural changes in the underlying manifold over time. The function $g(\cdot)$ may show change with respect to time that is constant ($g(t) = 0$), linear ($g(t) = t$), or quadratic ($g(t) = t^2$) with $\zeta$ serving as a scalar multiplier. The values $\upsilon_{\text{a}}$ and $\upsilon_{\text{p}}$ are randomly sampled from a normal distribution with mean zero and variance that is varied between simulation runs. Finally, the $\iota$ represent within-image noise in the high-dimensional space, and are randomly sampled from a normal distribution with mean zero. To account for the potential of spatially-dependent errors in the image segmentation process, the variance of this noise distribution varies according to a Gaussian process with mean 1 and using the squared exponential kernel, with the variance differing by simulation run and the bandwidth parameter set to 1. Note that the values $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can intuitively be considered size and shape parameters for the generated structure, respectively. Thus, variability in these terms represents measurement error that may result in misestimation of the structure size and shape. In addition to injecting variability in the estimated shapes of the simulated structures, we also allow the time points for all follow-up visits to vary according to a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 0.01, reflecting the fact that study visits in imaging studies do not occur at exact intervals in practice.}



\item {\it Your criterion function (4) includes terms with derivatives in space and another with a derivative in time, but no terms that include mixed partials in space and time.  I am wondering what are the implications of this choice and how much your result would change if you included some kind of integral of a squared mixed partial derivative in (4).  I'm not sure how far you would want to go down this avenue, but I do think it is at least worth acknowledging that the form of (4) is somewhat arbitrary and that other forms might be worth considering.  I would also note that (4) puts no penalty on large linear trends in time, which may not be biologically realistic.}

{\bf Response:}

The idea of including mixed partial derivatives in space and time in the criterion function is an interesting proposal which would have important implications for the interpretation of the minimization problem being addressed in this paper and the resulting embedding function. 

We made the decision to include derivatives with respect to space and time separately in the criterion function for several reasons. First, each of these derivatives individually represents a property of the expected behavior of manifolds in the neuroimaging setting: manifolds should display a certain level of smoothness (described by the spatial derivative), and because we are using the manifold to represent a structure in the brain, we expect changes in the manifold over time (represented by the temporal derivative). In the neuroimaging setting, we also found it advantageous to include these derivatives separately because the extent of a manifold's spatial smoothness may differ substantially from the extent of its temporal smoothness, and the two may not necessarily be related. For example, a function fitting to the surface of the hippocampus must show a relatively high level of curvature, but in a healthy aging individual this function should show minimal change over time. In this case, incorporating these partial derivatives separately allows us to effectively distinguish between spatial roughness and temporal smoothness, or vice versa.

By including mixed partial derivatives in space and time, the criterion function would effectively be penalizing joint change in space and time. While there are certainly situations in which this would be a valuable consideration, in this setting we expect that these mixed derivatives would work to counteract the separation between space and time that we believe to be useful since the disparities between spatial and temporal smoothness may be large. With this in mind, we expect that including the mixed partial derivatives would potentially result in meaningful changes in the results we yielded by the LPME algorithm. Namely, additional smoothing would be applied, mitigating any relatively quick changes in the estimated manifolds between nearby time points. Whether this additional smoothing is desireable would likely depend on the signal-to-noise ratio of the data: does the error in estimates of subcortical structures caused by the imaging and segmentation processes outweigh any real changes in the structure of interest? It is also unclear how much of the additional smoothing provided by including the additional mixed partial derivatives would not otherwise be included by increasing the tuning parameter values for either spatial or temporal smoothing in the LPME algorithm as proposed in the manuscript. An additional consideration is that the inclusion of mixed partial derivatives would likely make the algorithm more sensitive to the relationship between the units of time and space being used. While we standardized all dimensions in the observed data to fall between 0 and 1, it is not guaranteed that the roughness in time and space will occur over the same scale, even if it is present to some extent. This effect should be possible to avoid through careful preprocessing, but the possibility of dependency between spatial and temporal smoothness makes this preprocessing much more important for the success of the algorithm.

Finally, you note that the criterion function does not penalize large linear trends in time. We agree that these large linear trends in time are likely not biologically realistic. However, in our algorithmic approach, we attempted to place minimal assumptions on the form of the manifold and the temporal changes in that form. While we agree that we are unlikely to observe large linear changes in hippocampal structure over time, we feel we do not have enough prior information to explicitly encode those assumptions in the algorithm. The approach we have taken, which penalizes spatial and temporal roughness at a high level, remains flexible, accommodating a range of manifold forms while loosely encouraging biological plausibility.


\item {\it I recommend you add the application to the title of your paper, so something like "Longitudinal Principal Manifold Estimation for Modeling Changes Over Time in Brain Regions".}

{\bf Response:}

We appreciate your recommendation to include the application in the title of the paper, and we have revised the title as follows: ``\revision{Noise-corrected hippocampal shape and volume trajectories in Alzheimer's disease using longitudinal principal manifold estimation}".


\end{enumerate}


\large{\textbf{Response to Referee 1}}

\vspace{0.25in}

The paper proposes a novel method called Longitudinal Principal Manifold Estimation (LPME) to fit the spatial Principal Manifolds smoothed over time for longitudinal data. I have the following comments:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{In line 24 on page 3, it says "to develop smooth estimates of the surfaces of subcortical structures over time." Why not also consider cortical structure?}

{\bf Response:}
While we focused on subcortical structures in this work, the method we proposed could reasonably be applied to reach smooth estimates of cortical structure as well, and this may also yield interesting findings. Our focus on subcortical structures in this paper, and on the hippocampus in particular, was motivated by the popularity of hippocampal atrophy as a measure of Alzheimer's disease progression, and the known variability in automated segmentation methods. For these reasons, we believe our interest in describing subcortical structures will be highly relevant to the Alzheimer's disease research community.

\item \textit{In lines 30-31 on page 4, it says "the deep learning methodology used imposes costs in terms of computational requirements and interpretability". This sentence has a bias on deep learning (DL). Nowadays, GPU is almost a necessary device for brain image data analysis, due to powerful DL models in image classification and segmentation. Also, Google CoLab provides free access to GPU. DL models are very fast on GPU. For interpretability of DL, many tools have been developed. See the topic of Explainable AI.}

{\bf Response:} We agree, recent developments in deep learning methods have helped to alleviate some of the
computational burden and interpretability concerns associated with these approaches. However, deep learning methods
still typically require training data in quantities that are often unavailable for the neuroimaging settings in which we
are interested. While it may be possible to pretrain a model on a larger general-purpose dataset and fine-tune it using
data from a smaller Alzheimer's disease study population, model behavior in these circumstances is still difficult to
characterize. In any case, the approach developed by \cite{louis2019Riemannian} relies on assumptions relevant to the
disease progression context, limiting its utility for our application even if deep learning methods are appropriate in
this setting. We highlight a core assumption in the following
sentence added to the introduction:

\revision{However, this approach assumes that the progression trajectory for each subject must be parallel to a straight reference trajectory in some latent space, which may be overly restrictive in the case of heterogeneous conditions like AD.}

\item \textit{Line 15 on page 9 says "... by a study participant." Is this method used for the longitudinal data of a single subject, not simultaneously applied to a group of subjects?}

{\bf Response:}
Due to the extent of the differences in subcortical structures between individuals, in this work we applied the LPME algorithm to the longitudinal data of each subject individually. While group-level estimation is an important goal, we believe that in the neuroimaging setting it is effectively distinct from the goal of obtaining individual-level estimates and will thus require alternate approaches. 

Individual-level differences in brain structure have been found to be reliably present and relevant to behavioral outcomes, even among healthy individuals \citep{takao2015Brain, llera2019Interindividual}. This interest in individual-level variation transfers to studies of Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative conditions, which often display differences in disease progression and outcomes \citep{vogel2021Four}. The magnitude and hypothesized biological importance of these individual-level differences provide motivation for obtaining estimates of subcortical structures at the individual level.

Our work was also motivated by noise introduced during the image segmentation process, which is typically incorporated into larger image processing pipelines which are commonly conducted at the level of the image or individual, depending on how the images are registered for comparison. We believe that appplying the LPME algorithm at the individual level will allow it to integrate more flexibly with established pipelines. 

Again, while we focus here on individual-level estimates, we agree that group-level inference could help to answer a number of interesting research questions, and we plan to address this topic more fully in future work.

\item \textit{Also line 9 on page 10 says "for each image i". But the index i is from 1 to $I_t$, so the $x_it$ is an image, not just a 3D voxel? Is the method only for subject-level data analysis, not for group-level data analysis?}

  {\bf Response:} Thank you for the clarification. The index $i$ should denote each individual observation (voxel) $i$ within the image recorded at time point $t$. Thus, the algorithm is applied to point clouds of voxels available for each of the images recorded from a single individual. While group-level analysis is an important and relevant goal, in the neuroimaging setting, we expect that there is enough variability between individuals that a group-level analysis should more explicitly account for this variability to be effective. We seek to incorporate this consideration into our algorithmic framework in future work. We have corrected our discussion of the notation used in the paper as follows:

\revision{Let $\left\{x_{it}\right\}_{i=1, t=1}^{I_t, T}$ represent the $I = \sum_{t=1}^{T}I_t$ observations in $\mathbb{R}^D$ (i.e., each $x_{it}\in\mathbb{R}^D$) for each participant, where $I_t$ denotes the total number of observations available within the image recorded at time point $t$, with $T$ total time points.}


\item \textit{In Eq. (3), (t,r) should be (r, t) since the domain is $R^d x R$ not $R x R^d$.}

{\bf Response:}

Thank you for the correction. We have incorporated the change in the equation notation accordingly.

\item \textit{In figure 3, the plots for time=0.3 \& 0.6, the true manifold (red curve) doesn’t well align with the scatter plot. Is it because the noise is too strong? And the plot for time =0.9, the LPME (blue curve) is two-mode, not as smooth as the other 2 methods and the true manifold.}

{\bf Response:} In the cases you highlighted, the discrepancies between the true manifold and the observed data are due to high levels of noise. While noise to this extent is likely not present in the imaging data we are directly interested in, we sought to understand how the LPME method would perform in cases where this level of noise is present. Regarding the plot when time = 0.9, we cannot say with certainty why the LPME estimate is bi-modal or shows less smoothness than the other methods, but it is likely that these characteristics result from the temporal smoothing, which will cause the LPME estimate to incorporate information from adjacent time points, which were not shown in this figure due to space constraints. Note that since the the simulations have been rerun with an updated data generation process to address other comments, figure 3 has been changed to reflect the new results.


\item \textit{In line 19-26 on page 25, why only consider the 463 subjects from the total 800 subjects of ADNI 1?}

  {\bf Response:} To download the ADNI data, we rely on the Image \& Data Archive, run by the Laboratory of Neuro Imaging at the USC Mark and Mary Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute. We ran a search for all T1-weighted MRI images from the ADNI 1 study, and limited our analysis to images with descriptions containing ``MPRAGE" or ``MP-RAGE", as these images were taken according to the same specifications and provide a high resolution view of the structures of interest. However, this search did not include images for all subjects enrolled in the study, resulting in the discrepancy. We have since incorporated data from the ADNI 2 and ADNI 3 cohorts as well, with similar differences between the number of subjects for whom MP-RAGE images are available and the total number subjects enrolled in the study. We have updated Section 5 of the paper to describe this reasoning as follows: 

\revision{While the original ADNI 1 cohort included 200 cognitively healthy elderly individuals,
400 with mild cognitive impairment, and 200 with AD, this analysis focuses on 463 par-
ticipants for whom T1-weighted MP-RAGE images were available through the study's online repository, located at \href{https://ida.loni.usc.edu/login.jsp?project=ADNI}{\texttt{https://ida.loni.usc.edu/login.jsp?project=ADNI}}. Of these participants, 130 were cognitively normal, 223 displayed symptoms of mild cognitive impairment, and 101 were diagnosed with AD at the baseline study visit.}


\item \textit{In line 27 on page 25, it says "The LPME method was applied to the 236 subjects from the three groups, CN, MCI, and AD, for image segmentation." But the LPME is for longitudinal data analysis, not for image segmentation.}

{\bf Response:}

\question{I have checked the manuscript and I have been unable to find the quoted sentence. It appears that the comment takes issue with the implication that LPME is being used for image segmentation, and I would be happy to change the wording if that were the case, but I'm not sure what the best way to proceed is.}

\item \textit{Line 5 on page 25 says "...meaningful between-image errors … are introduced ..." Is "meaningful" a typo?}

{\bf Response:} We had intended this sentence to reiterate the fact that the image segmentation process results in error that meaningfully impacts estimates of subcortical structures. To clarify this message, we have changed the sentence as follows:

As discussed previously, \revision{substantial} between-image errors in estimates of subcortical structures are introduced during the process of segmenting MRI images.

\item \textit{For the ADNI data, you used 236 subjects from the three groups, CN, MCI, and AD,  for image segmentation, but used LPME to fit each subject individually? Where is the comparison between the 3 groups CN, AD, and MCI?}

{\bf Response:} As discussed in greater detail above, our work for this paper focused on the development of the LPME algorithm and its application at the individual level. Because of this, we do not explicitly compare the three study groups included here. Rather, the study groups are included to indicate that the LPME algorithm is applicable across these groups, which display different characteristics both at baseline and longitudinally.

\item \textit{Timing performance of the proposed method is needed for simulation and real-data analysis.}

{\bf Response:}

Thank you for the suggestion. Our updated simulation results include the time required to fit the relevant models in the simulation study and the applied analysis.

\large{\textbf{Response to Referee 2}}

\vspace{0.25in}

{\it The authors of this manuscript propose and evaluate a longitudinal principal manifold estimation method and a novel data augmentation approach to enable principal manifold estimation on self-intersecting manifolds. The developed methodology is relatively computationally efficient and provides good approximation for the changes in the brain volume structures. The proposed method, LPME, is also applied to the data obtained from the longitudinal ADNI study.  The work undertaken is interesting and the new method and its application to the ADNI study data is a worthwhile undertaking. However, there are a few issues that should be addressed. First, the described methodology depends on a relatively strong assumption that the underlying manifold is minimally changing over time and the changes can be well described by a smooth time function. Second, it is not clear if the method proposed applies only to discrete time points or if it can be used in the continuous time setting, i.e. with irregular time intervals. Third, the simulation studies summarized indicate that the new method is advantageous is specific simulation settings. Are such settings relevant to the brain structure changes? Forth, is it feasible to estimate smooth changes over time with a very limited number of longitudinal time points. Please find additional comments below.}

Specific comments

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Abstract:    Need to be more specific about the type of the MRI used. I believe the authors mean the structural MRI (sMRI).}

{\bf Response:}
You are correct, we did use structural MRI data in this work. To ensure this is clear, we included this information in
the first sentence of the abstract.

\item \textit{Abstract:    The plural of “hippocampus” is “hippocampi.”}

 {\bf Response:}
 Thank you for the clarification. We have corrected the plural of ``hippocampus" as ``\revision{hippocampi}" in the abstract, and changed the plural form of ``thalamus" to ``\revision{thalami}" for consistency.

\item \textit{Introduction, p.2:  References on processing of the sMRI data are dated. There has been a lot of progress in the past decade in the sMRI processing pipelines.}

{\bf Response:}
While sMRI processing pipelines have certainly progressed in recent years, our review of the relevant literature
indicates that similar issues persist. For example, \cite{akudjedu2018comparative} and \cite{quilis-sancho2020comparative} each consider the accuracy or self-consistency of segmentation methods for sMRI data, finding that consistency tends to vary by brain region, with performance for the hippocampus lower than other regions. We have included the following sentences in the introduction to reflect this information:

\revision{Similarly, \cite{akudjedu2018comparative} found that FIRST and FreeSurfer showed strong correlation with manual segmentation for the caudate, but relatively poor absolute agreement and moderate relative consistency when applied to the hippocampus. When comparing estimated subcortical volumes obtained using FIRST and FreeSurfer in two consecutive yearly study visits, \cite{quilis-sancho2020comparative} found that considerable variability was present in the relative differences in subcortical volume estimates, even after removing outliers from analysis. Furthermore, this variability was much greater for FIRST than FreeSurfer, with standard deviations for relative volumetric difference in the left and right hippocampus reaching 12.2\% and 11.3\%, respectively \citep{quilis-sancho2020comparative}.}

\item \textit{Introduction, p.5: Are the monotonicity restrictions included in the modeling? For example, volumes of the brain structures should not increase over time.}

{\bf Response:}
We have not included any monotonicity constraints in the modeling. Throughout the process of developing our methods, we sought to capture the characteristics of the data without imposing assumptions that would make it difficult to use a similar approach in other settings. For example, while we would not expect the volume of a brain structure to increase over time in the aging population we consider here, the same would not be true in a child development dataset.

Even for someone with Alzheimer's disease, including a strict monotonicity restriction could overemphasize the images collected at the study's baseline visit or at other early visits, which themselves may underestimate the volume of the structure of interest. By taking the unconstrained smoothing approach we propose, we hope to mitigate most of these unwanted effects in practice while retaining flexibility to overcome this sort of situation.

\item \textit{Section 3, p.9:    Justification for the use of manifolds to learn about the volume changes is not fully satisfactory as volumes are scalar quantities. One does not need to estimate the surface changes in order to obtain a volume of a 3D object.}

{\bf Response:}
We agree, it is not necessary to estimate the surface of a structure to approximate its volume. In this sense, the manifold estimation approach we propose in this paper is more complicated than is needed for the problem we identify. However, estimating the surface structure has benefits beyond volume estimation that may prove valuable for other problems. Namely, the volume of a subcortical structure like the hippocampus is a blunt summary of its shape, while estimating the surface of the structure provides a much more informative description. Given the importance of the hippocampus in functions related to memory and learning, we believe that characterizing \textit{how} the hippocampus atrophies in those with Alzheimer's disease may be as important as quantifying the extent of this atrophy, and the manifold estimates we obtain are better suited to answering this question. Due to the greater focus on hippocampal volume at the present time, we decided to address a problem with a direct impact on this line of work while establishing an approach that can easily extend to more informative measures of hippocampal change in future work.


\item \textit{Section 3.1, p.11: Description of the minimization problem is unclear.}

{\bf Response:}

\question{I feel like this comment is pretty broadly stated, and it isn't clear to me whether there is anything specific the reviewer would like more clarity on, or if they feel the entire minimization problem itself is ill-posed.}


\item \textit{Section 3.2.2:    The choice of the first time point for the initialization is arbitrary and prone to error. Why the longitudinal information is not used in the initialization?}

{\bf Response:} Because the parameterization obtained in the initialization step is iteratively improved through the remaining steps of the LPME algorithm, we had used the first time point to balance the quality of the initialization with the computational burden of obtaining that initialization. However, this choice was indeed arbitrary, so we have updated the algorithm to use the available data from all time points to obtain the initialization. Without formally comparing the time required to obtain initializations under both scenarios, we found that while including data from all time points did increase the time needed to reach an initial parameterization, this increase in time was inconsequential relative to the computational cost of the remainder of the LPME algorithm.


\item \textit{Table 2, p.23 and Table S1: While in many simulation settings (Cases), the LPME algorithm seems to perform well. Case 7 looks to be troublesome. Any specific reason why in this case PC/PS method performs the best on average?}

{\bf Response:} In the previously submitted simulation results, we included some simulation runs with very high levels of noise that are likely unrealistic in an applied setting, with the goal of demonstrating where the LPME algorithm is able to obtain an adequete fit to the data, and when it begins to struggle. Simulation case 7 uses the Swiss Roll manifold to generate the data, and sufficient levels of noise can cause the structural integrity of the manifold to degrade. We believe that the principal surface method performed better than the PME-based methods in these settings, but do not expect those results to be highly relevant in an applied setting. More broadly, the relative performance of manifold learning methods changes with the properties of the manifolds to which they are applied. While the PME and LPME algorithms are extensions of the principal surface method, there are differences in the implementation of the methods (e.g. the Isomap initialization used by PME and LPME) that may cause one to outperform the other in a given setting.

In the revised manuscript, we have adjusted the simulation parameters to keep noise levels closer to what may realistically be observed in neuroimaging data. While this does not make the limits of the performance of the LPME algorithm as clear, we believe it presents a more realistic picture of how LPME will perform in comparison to the alternative methods. LPME tends to show the greatest improvement in performance over the alternative methods when high levels of between-image noise are present, so by limiting the extent of the noise between time points, we believe the revised simulations understate the improvements of the LPME method compared to the original simulation approach.


\item \textit{Figure 2, p.24:    The figure is difficult to interpret as the colors for LPME and Principal Curves are similar and the symbols are hard to differentiate among the methods.}

  {\bf Response:} We agree that the figure is difficult to interpret. We have made changes to ensure that it is easier to differentiate between the methods being used. \question{I am still working on making these changes, but they should be available for the simulation results shortly.}

\end{enumerate}
